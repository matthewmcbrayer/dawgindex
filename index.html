What is this?

Vegas sets the bar for every game. We grade who cleared it.

  1. What it is. Every Power 4 game is graded against the closing line. DAWG is how many points better or worse than expected a team played, with garbage time thrown out. 0 = exactly what Vegas expected · +12.3 or better = a DAWG game · −12.3 or worse = SOFT
  2. Why it matters. A record says who won. This says who played above or below what was expected of them. PLAY checks the scoreboard snap by snap, in the same points.
  3. Why trust it. Every Power 4 game since 2019, every grade public, and the formula is frozen and versioned. We show what holds up and what doesn't.

Quarterbacks

Points per game each QB adds compared with an average Power 4 QB

Every pass, sack, run and turnover counts, adjusted for the defense he faced. Unlike team scores, this one sticks from year to year, even after a transfer.

What counts. Each of his plays is valued by how much it changed the expected score, using the open-source cfbfastR expected-points model (the same one behind PLAY): a 3rd-down conversion adds, a sack or a pick in his own end costs a lot. Overtime snaps are left out because that model doesn't cover overtime. Each play is then compared with what that defense gave up to every QB that season, so 7 yards against Georgia counts for more than 7 against a bad defense. Blowouts (24+ points) and FCS games are left out.

The rating. Shown as points per game against an average Power 4 QB, on a typical 30 plays. A few games can swing wildly, so a QB with only a handful of games is pulled toward average until more games come in. "Raw" is the same number with no pulling.

Does it hold up? Yes, more than anything else on this site. A QB's number carries over to his next season at about 0.5 (0 = not at all, 1 = perfectly), and about the same after a transfer. Team season DAWG carries over at about 0. No big-game or clutch splits. We tested them (big games, and 3rd/4th down or 4th quarter within 8): a QB's split one year says almost nothing about the next, so no QB has shown a repeatable big-game or clutch trait, and we don't show one. Game logs still mark which games were big stages, as a description of the game.

What it can't separate. His line, receivers and play-calling are part of every play. When a team swaps QBs, its new QB's number still carries over about 0.3 from the old one, so some of this is the program around him.

How the Grade Works

Two numbers for every team in every Power 4 game since 2019, kept separate on purpose. Tap any heading below for the full rule.

DAWG

The scoreboard against the closing line: how many points better or worse than expected a team played, with garbage time thrown out.

2

The same question, snap by snap: how well the team moved the ball and stopped it, turned into points.

3

Labels

+12.3 or better is a DAWG game, +24 a Certified DAWG. −12.3 or worse is SOFT, −24 Certified SOFT. Labels come from DAWG only.

Then: a team's season number is the plain average of its games. No extra weight for road games, night games or ranked opponents, and DAWG and PLAY are never added together.

DAWG

Take the final margin with garbage time removed. Compare it with the margin teams usually produce at that closing spread. A 7-point favorite usually wins by about 7.6 once garbage time is out; a 35-point favorite by only about 29, because blowouts get called off early. DAWG is the difference, in points.

DAWG = margin (no garbage time) − expected margin for that spread

Garbage time is out

A play is garbage time when the lead before the snap is more than 38 in the 2nd quarter, 28 in the 3rd or 22 in the 4th. That's the CollegeFootballData rule. The 1st quarter and overtime are never garbage time. If the lead shrinks back under the line, plays count again, so a collapse still shows up. DAWG and PLAY use the same window.

PLAY

Every competitive scrimmage snap is valued by how much it changed the expected score (expected points added). A team's number is its offense's points added per play minus what its defense allowed per play. PLAY compares that with what the spread implied and turns it into points, scaled by how many competitive snaps the game had, so it reads on the same scale as DAWG.

PLAY = conversion × (net EPA per play − expected for that spread) × (competitive snaps ÷ average snaps)

It's shown as a whole number because one game's snaps are noisy: a single game's PLAY is good to about ±12 points. A season of games is much steadier.

Known lean. PLAY runs a little low for clear favorites. From 2019 to 2026, teams favored by 14 to 21 averaged −1.7 PLAY and teams favored by 21 to 28 averaged −2.0, where a fair grade would average 0 (DAWG for the same games averages within half a point of 0). That's small next to one game's ±12, but over a season a big favorite's PLAY reads about 2 points low. V3's formula is frozen, so this isn't adjusted mid-version; read a heavy favorite's PLAY with that in mind.

DAWG and PLAY are separate

They usually agree. When they don't, it's because PLAY only counts scrimmage snaps: kickoffs, punts, field goals, return touchdowns and how the big plays happened to bunch together move the scoreboard but not PLAY. We show both and let the disagreement speak for itself. We never add them, average them or rank by both.

The labels

The cutoffs are the 5th, 20th, 80th and 95th percentiles of every Power 4 game from 2014 to 2022, rounded to the same distance on both sides, then frozen. About 1 game in 20 is Certified DAWG and 1 in 20 Certified SOFT; most games are As expected.

No hindsight

Each season is graded with expectations built only from earlier seasons, back to 2014. The 2026 grades use what was learned from 2014–2025; the 2019 grades use 2014–2018. Once a season's numbers are set, they don't change.

Missing data

No closing line: no DAWG and no PLAY. If the scoring record doesn't match the final score, no DAWG. No usable play-by-play: DAWG comes from ESPN's scoring summary and PLAY isn't available. We check the play-by-play's scoring order against ESPN's scoring summary. If the two disagree, DAWG uses the summary and PLAY isn't shown, because the play-by-play's score columns are out of order. If the play-by-play's scoring doesn't add up and there's no summary to check it against, no DAWG. Fewer than 20 competitive snaps on offense or on defense: PLAY shows "too few plays." Overtime points count in DAWG, but overtime snaps aren't in PLAY, because the expected-points model doesn't cover overtime.

What's description only

Cash-in after stops, Answers, Knife twist, turnovers and the setting (road, night, packed stadium, ranked opponent) are shown as counts and tags. They describe a game; none of them changes a grade.

What changed from V2

The old grade averaged five scored parts and multiplied the result for tougher settings. That's gone, along with the big-game split, Luck and the Matchup Builder: the research didn't support them as lasting team traits. The old site is archived, unchanged, at dawgindex.com/v2.

Questions You'll Ask

Why isn't my unbeaten team ranked higher?

The rank isn't about wins. It's how a team has played compared with what was expected of it. A 5–0 team that was supposed to win big and keeps winning close can rank below a 3–1 team that keeps beating expectations. Records still matter for the playoff. This measures something different: who's showing up.

Is this a betting tool?

No. DAWG and PLAY describe games after they happen. A team's season DAWG barely carries into the next season (the numbers are in "Does It Hold Up?" above), so it isn't a forecast of anything.

Why don't DAWG and PLAY always agree?

DAWG is the scoreboard. PLAY is the snaps. Kicks, returns and how the big plays bunched together count on the scoreboard but not in PLAY, so a team can win big on special teams with ordinary snaps, or play well snap to snap and lose the kicking game. When they disagree, both numbers are telling you something true.

Why does a win sometimes grade negative?

Because DAWG compares against expectations. A team favored by 20 that wins by 10 didn't do what it was supposed to, and DAWG says so. Same the other way: a 7-point loss as a 20-point underdog grades well.

Does the formula change during the season?

No. The formula is frozen and versioned (now v3.0). Before each season, its expectations are built from earlier seasons only and then locked. Any other change ships as a new version with a dated entry below, and old grades keep the version they were made with. Data fixes are listed below too, so nothing changes quietly.
