      <p class="lede">The last play of the game was Oregon's seventh turnover.</p>
      <p>Down 17–15 with four seconds left and the ball at its own 37, Oregon needed something ridiculous. Brock Thomas found Evan Stewart, Stewart got loose past midfield and pitched it, and the play ended with UCLA on the ball. That's the one giveaway nobody should hold against the Ducks. It was a last-gasp lateral.</p>
      <p>The other six are a different story. Four fumbles and two interceptions, in a game Oregon closed as a 10-point favorite at Autzen Stadium.</p>
      <p>Here's what makes this one strange: Oregon lost by two. Teams that turn it over seven times don't lose by two. Since 2014, in games involving today's Power 4 teams, 17 teams have done it. All 17 lost, and the other 16 lost by a median of 28 points. Oregon came within one two-point conversion of a tie.</p>

      <div class="pull">Seven turnovers.<br><span class="r">Two points.</span></div>

      <h2>Where the seven went</h2>
      <p>Both teams were on backup quarterbacks by the end. Oregon starter Dante Moore missed the game with a concussion from the USC game two weeks earlier, so Dylan Raiola started. Raiola threw for 43 yards, with two interceptions and a lost fumble, and Dan Lanning went to third-stringer Brock Thomas at quarterback in the fourth quarter. UCLA's Nico Iamaleava left at halftime with a lower-body injury, and Ty Dieffenbach finished the game.</p>
      <p>With that backdrop, Oregon's offense had 89 yards through three quarters. Here's every giveaway and what UCLA did with the ball afterward.</p>

      <p>Two things stand out. First, UCLA got three short fields, starting at the Oregon 41, 33 and 33, and turned them into seven points: an interception in the end zone, a fourth-down stop at the 3 and one touchdown. Second, the most expensive giveaway wasn't one UCLA scored off at all. Oregon had first-and-10 at the UCLA 13, already leading 6–3, when Jordon Davison lost the ball. In expected-points terms, that one play cost Oregon more than five points before UCLA ever touched the ball.</p>

      <h2>So how did Oregon lose by only two?</h2>
      <p><strong>UCLA gave three back.</strong> Iamaleava lost nine yards and the ball at his own 1 in the first quarter, and Oregon scored on the next snap. Late in the first half, he threw interceptions on back-to-back possessions, the second one picked off by Koi Perich in the end zone with 23 seconds left.</p>
      <p><strong>Oregon's defense kept making plays.</strong> Matayo Uiagalelei blocked a 27-yard field goal. The Ducks stopped Wayne Knight on fourth-and-2 at their own 3. UCLA outgained Oregon 345 yards to 272 and still finished with 17 points.</p>
      <p><strong>It still came down to two-point tries.</strong> Oregon went for two after both of its touchdowns and didn't convert either. Make one and the game is tied.</p>

      <p>On the 122 snaps that weren't turnovers, the two teams were almost exactly even. Neither offense did much. The turnovers were the game.</p>
      <p>That cuts both ways for Oregon. The giveaways were the difference, but "even on the clean snaps" isn't a good result for a 10-point home favorite. A 10-point favorite is supposed to be the better team on most snaps. On the clean ones, Oregon wasn't, with its second- and third-string quarterbacks playing.</p>

Teams with exactly six won 4 of 44, most recently SMU, which beat Duke 28–27 in overtime in 2024. Seven has never worked, and Saturday was as close as it has gotten.</p>

      <h2>What the DAWG numbers say</h2>
      <p>DAWG grades the scoreboard against what the closing line expected. UCLA won by two as a 10-point underdog, which is <strong>+12.7, a DAWG game</strong>. Oregon gets the mirror image, −12.7, SOFT.</p>
      <p>PLAY, which grades the snaps, is harsher: <strong>+16.8 for UCLA, −16.8 for Oregon</strong>. That's the turnovers again. The scoreboard says Oregon nearly escaped. The snaps say it was worse than the score, and the only reason it wasn't worse still is that UCLA kept handing some of it back.</p>

      <h2>This didn't start Saturday</h2>
      <p>For UCLA, it's more of the same. The Bruins are 5–0, first among 68 Power 4 teams in PLAY and second in DAWG, and the defense has 18 takeaways in five games, the most in the Power 4. They were unranked until last week. They're No. 12 now.</p>
      <p>Oregon has been on the other end most of the year. The worst game of its season came before the quarterback injury: a 39–31 loss at Oklahoma State as a 24.5-point favorite, with Moore playing. Raiola came on against USC and Oregon won, about as expected. Then Saturday.</p>

      <h2>So what did we learn?</h2>
      <p><strong>About Oregon:</strong> less than the turnover count suggests, and more than you'd like if you're a Ducks fan. Seven giveaways in one game isn't who Oregon is. It had one in its first four games. But a 10-point favorite breaking even on the clean snaps fits a season in which Oregon has fallen short of its line in three of five games, and hasn't beaten it by enough to earn a DAWG grade once.</p>
      <p><strong>About UCLA:</strong> its defense has been taking the ball away all season, and it found a way to win at Autzen for the first time since 2004 with its backup quarterback finishing the game. It didn't need the offense to be good, just to be there when Oregon opened the door.</p>
      <p>Seventeen teams have turned it over seven or more times. Seventeen have lost. Oregon is the only one that lost by less than a touchdown.</p>

      <aside class="doesnt" aria-label="What this doesn't tell us">
        <h3>What this doesn't tell us</h3>
        <ul>
          <li><strong>Turnovers are noisy.</strong> Fumbles especially swing on bounces. One seven-turnover game says little about how Oregon will handle the ball the rest of the way.</li>
          <li><strong>Backup quarterbacks.</strong> Oregon played without its starter, then went to its third-stringer. This isn't a read on Oregon's full-strength offense.</li>
          <li><strong>Small samples.</strong> Oregon's season PLAY (−12.9) is four graded games. Random swings alone put a four-game average anywhere within about ±10 points of zero (a 95% range), and with 68 teams, a few land outside that range by chance every week. Oregon is outside it, which isn't proof of anything lasting.</li>
          <li><strong>One game's PLAY is noisy.</strong> Single-game PLAY results vary a lot: about 95% of them land within roughly ±21 points, and the standard deviation is about 11 points. Saturday's +16.8 and −16.8 are inside that range.</li>
          <li><strong>Expected points are a model.</strong> The split above uses the same open-source model as PLAY. It measures the value of field position, down and distance, not the quality of each decision.</li>
          <li><strong>History's coverage.</strong> "Since 2014" means games involving today's 68 Power 4 teams. Turnover counts come from ESPN box scores.</li>
        </ul>
      </aside>
